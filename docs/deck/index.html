So why didn't this exist already? It's not because any one piece was missing.

Here's the honest state of the agent economy. Payment is solved — x402 gives agents a way to pay per request. Execution is solved — TEEs let a machine sign "I ran this." Reputation is solved — ERC-8004 gives agents identities you can look up. Three good pieces. But look at the gaps. A TEE signature says a computation happened in here. It says nothing about which computation you ordered. So if I pay an agent for a market analysis and it runs something cheaper and hands me a signed output, the signature still verifies. The attestation is real. The job is wrong. That's the hole — and it bites the moment no human opens the PDF.

And that hole gets expensive exactly when agents get useful.

Why now

The premise of agent commerce is that nobody opens the PDF.

Human in the loop — someone re-reads the deliverable
Agent to agent — nobody checks the deliverable
Substitution gets caughtSubstitution is free money
  • A human client re-reads the work. An agent client cannot. That reader was the whole quality control layer, and the roadmap deletes it.
  • Substituted work is cheaper work. The incentive is structural, not hypothetical.
  • Autonomous payment means autonomous fraud — at machine scale, machine speed.
  • Reputation anchored to unverified delivery launders bad work into a good score.

Today this doesn't bite, because a human opens the PDF. But the entire premise of agent commerce is that no human opens the PDF. The instant you remove that reader, task substitution becomes free money — do the cheap job, deliver the signed output, collect. And the reputation layer makes it worse, not better, because feedback anchored to unverified deliveries just launders bad work into a good score. So: we don't need another payment rail and we don't need another TEE. We need the wire between them.

The wire turned out to be one number.

The idea

One signed hash, carried all the way through.

intentHash = keccak256(brief, data, constraints, price, nonce) — signed EIP-712 by Alice. It is now the identity of the job.

0x7f3a9c…4e12
01 · Sign Alice EIP-712 over everything that defines the job
02 · Pay x402 The same hash authorizes the payment
03 · Enclave 0G Sealed Inference Goes in with the prompt — comes back inside the TEE-signed body
04 · Verdict Verifier.sol Recovered on-chain before anything is emitted

0G attests the compute. We attest the intent.

Alice hashes everything that defines her job — the brief, the data, the constraints, the price, a nonce — and signs it. That hash is now the identity of the job. It authorizes the payment. It goes into the enclave at the top of the prompt. It comes back out inside the response that the TEE signs. And it's the value the contract recovers before it will emit a verdict. So the guarantee isn't "a TEE produced an output." It's "a TEE produced this output for this order, and here is the signature chain." We call it intent-bound verification. One hash is the whole trick — and it's the reason our verdict means something the other three layers can't say on their own.

Four networks, and I want to be precise about why each one is here, because "we used the sponsors" is not an architecture. 0G runs the model in a sealed enclave and signs the output — that's the compute. The Graph is how Alice finds Bob at all; she starts with no address, queries by skill, and ranks candidates by deliveries a contract confirmed. Base holds the verdict: our Verifier recovers two independent signatures — Bob's binding signature and Alice's own EIP-712 intent — and refuses to emit unless the hashes match. And Hedera does two things: it carries the money, with settlement gated on the verdict, and its consensus service timestamps the whole off-chain timeline so the ordering isn't our word against anyone's. Nothing here duplicates anything else. Every identifier on this slide is live on testnet right now.

Two things I'll flag for the technical judges. First: 0G's signature does not cover the answer text — it covers a tuple containing the sha256 of the raw response body. We found that by hashing every candidate against the digest until one matched, and it changes how you build on it: you hash raw bytes, because a JSON round-trip reorders keys and breaks the signature silently. Second, that's exactly what lets us bind: put the intent hash at the top of the prompt, the model echoes it, and now the enclave's signature covers a body containing Alice's hash. Five out of five verbatim, and it's a CI gate, not a claim. The whole thing runs in twenty-one seconds median against our sixty-second budget.

Now let me tell you the one thing we can't prove yet — because we'd rather you hear it from us.

What we didn't fake

0G attests the compute and carries the intent through it. The match check is client-verifiable — not yet third-party-verifiable.

What is real

The first link in the chain comes from actual 0G hardware. Bob cannot forge it on his own machine.

Alice can verify the match herself, independently, from the response body.

What is not

The code that decides match runs on an ordinary host. We had no TDX machine and 0G does not host enclave execution for us.

A stranger cannot yet verify match without trusting us.

We could have shipped a screenshot of an attestation. Instead: attestation: 'none' and imageHash: null are in the code and in the UI until a TDX host changes it. The delta is one machine — nothing else in the pipeline moves.

One gap, and we're naming it ourselves. The code that decides whether the hashes match runs on an ordinary server, not inside an enclave — we had no TDX machine this weekend. We could have shipped a screenshot of an attestation and most people would never check. Instead we moved one end of the binding into 0G's real enclave, so the first link in the chain comes from actual hardware. What that gives you: Alice can verify the match herself. What it doesn't: a stranger can't, yet. That's one machine away, and it changes nothing else in the pipeline. We built a verification product — it would be a strange thing to lie in.
